# Train juan_v3 to step, not switch

Don't add a separate recovery policy that a supervisor switches to when the IMU tilt passes 10 to 15 degrees. For a robot Juan's size, the best-supported approach is to keep one deployed network: fine-tune juan_v3 under a push curriculum and domain randomization, and anchor it to the original gait with a KL or behavior-cloning penalty. The tilt threshold still has two jobs. During training it can label which teacher to imitate. At runtime it can trigger a safe-fall or freeze mode once a fall can no longer be avoided. Every hardware-proven small biped in the notes uses a single robust walking policy whose push recovery comes from randomization and perturbation training: Open Duck Mini, ToddlerBot, Disney BDX, DeepMind's OP3, Cassie and Booster T1. Where teams did train separate skills, the best small-biped result (OP3) distilled them back into one network rather than switching at runtime. The physics points the same way. Juan's center of mass sits about 0.25 m high, so its balance error grows with a time constant of about 0.16 s. By the time the torso has tilted 10 degrees, the center of mass is already about as far forward as the edge of a typical small foot, so only a step can save it. A switch that fires at that point hands control to a policy that has never seen the walker's state, in the middle of the one maneuver the walker most needs to be doing itself. The 52% fall rate also needs context: the 0.3 m/s test push sits close to what any controller could physically recover from on this robot. Some of those falls are physics, and the best returns probably lie in actuator, latency and IMU fidelity rather than in a new architecture.

## Separate stay-upright policies have no biped hardware track record

The literature uses runtime switching almost exclusively to change *goals*, not to keep walking. ANYmal's hierarchy of three behavior policies plus a learned behavior selector recovered from falls in under 5 s in more than 97% of over 100 trials, but those behaviors were self-righting, standing up and walking, not staying upright mid-stride ([Lee et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.07517)). SafeFall on the Unitree G1 does run a GRU fall predictor alongside the walker. It hands control to a separate PPO policy only when a fall is *unavoidable*, and that policy's job is to land softly. It cut peak impact by 22.1% on hardware, and gentle pushes that the walker could recover from deliberately did not trigger it ([SafeFall](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18509)). Standing Tall predicted falls on Digit about 1.1 s ahead but built no recovery controller at all, and could not measure hardware false positives ([Standing Tall](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01141)). Across these sources, no biped with hardware results switches from an RL walker to a separate learned stay-upright policy and beats a single robust walker.

The successful small and mid-size bipeds instead put push recovery inside the walking policy. OP3 is the closest match to Juan: 51 cm and 3.5 kg, with hobby-class Dynamixel servos. Trained without domain randomization and perturbations, it fell on hardware "with every one or two steps." Random torso impulses, targeted dynamics randomization and an upright-torso reward (within 11.5 degrees) produced a policy that "reacts to prevent falling in the first instance" ([Haarnoja et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.13653)). Disney's BDX character "deviates from the reference trajectory and contact schedule to recover" from pushes, and logged about **10 hours of public deployment without a single fall** ([Grandia et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204)). Booster's T1 is hit by a 10 kg ball dropped from 1 m and "regains stability within a few steps" with one end-to-end policy ([Booster Gym](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15132)). Cassie's walking policy steps to recover and returns to standing even without perturbation training, while standing-only policies fall once leaned past the support region. That is a direct warning against a balance-only specialist that cannot step ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.16889)).

Switching also has documented failure modes. In a quadruped study, a baseline that switched abruptly between separately trained experts "often failed... likely due to distribution mismatches," and failures concentrated "during transitions between distinct skills" ([arXiv 2607.13579](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.13579)). Discrete skill switching on another quadruped "failed to transition to galloping in the real world," while learned blending worked ([arXiv 2502.06676](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.06676v1)). For Juan, four problems compound:

- The recovery policy is invoked in states drawn from the walker's failures.
- The walker is handed back post-recovery states it never trained on.
- Joint setpoints jump at the handoff.
- The threshold forces a trade-off. Trigger late and the recoverer inherits doomed states. Trigger early and you steal control from a walker that would have recovered. SafeFall's unmasked predictor bought 1.22 s of warning but at a 4.1% false-alarm rate. Masking the ambiguous region cut false alarms to 0.06%, and lead time fell to 0.41 s ([SafeFall](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18509)).

Disney does switch between separate standing and walking policies. It makes this work only by switching at double support and by feeding every policy its previous two actions ([Grandia et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204)). A tilt-triggered switch fires at exactly the wrong phase: mid-disturbance, usually in single support.

| Option | Hardware precedent on bipeds | Risk to the current gait | Fit for Juan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Walker + tilt-triggered recovery policy | None found for staying upright; only for get-up or safe-fall | Low in calm walking, high at handoffs | Poor: the trigger fires after the step decision is due |
| Single robust policy (fine-tuned juan_v3) | OP3, BDX, Cassie, Booster T1, Open Duck | Manageable with KL/BC anchoring | Best first move |
| Teachers distilled into one student | OP3 (walk/kick + get-up), FIRM on G1 | Low; the walker is an explicit teacher | Good second move if fine-tuning plateaus |
| Runtime supervisor for unavoidable falls | SafeFall (G1), BDX motion-stop | None if tuned conservatively | Add it, but as damage control |

## At 10 to 15 degrees the robot must already be stepping

Capture-point arithmetic shows why a tilt-only trigger is too late on Juan. With a center-of-mass height h = 0.25 m, the inverted-pendulum constant is ω = √(g/h) ≈ 6.26 s⁻¹, so balance error diverges with a time constant of about **0.16 s** ([push recovery notes, from ICP dynamics in IHMC's patent](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8942848)). A sudden 0.3 m/s push moves the instantaneous capture point (where the foot must land to stop) by Δv/ω ≈ **4.8 cm**. That is already beyond a typical small-robot foot half-length of 3 to 4 cm. At a static 10 degree tilt, the center of mass is about 4.3 cm past the ankle (my computation: 0.25 m × sin 10°), and about 6.5 cm at 15 degrees. Ankle torque alone cannot hold either. Any tilt rate makes it worse.

The table below comes from an idealized linear-pendulum recursion in the notes. It suggests ankle and hip strategy alone caps out near 0.19 to 0.25 m/s. Recovering 0.3 m/s from standing needs one step landing within about 0.2 to 0.25 s. Swing time matters more than step length: cutting it from 0.25 s to 0.15 s raises one-step capacity by roughly 30%.

| Push-to-touchdown Δt | Step length | Foot half-length | Max Δv, no step | 1 step | 3 steps |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.15 s | 8 cm | 3 cm | 0.19 m/s | 0.38 m/s | 0.49 m/s |
| 0.20 s | 10 cm | 4 cm | 0.25 m/s | 0.43 m/s | 0.49 m/s |
| 0.25 s | 8 cm | 3 cm | 0.19 m/s | 0.29 m/s | 0.32 m/s |
| 0.30 s | 10 cm | 3 cm | 0.19 m/s | 0.28 m/s | 0.30 m/s |

The model ignores swing-leg limits ([arXiv 1601.06616](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/1601.06616)). Pushes during walking add to an existing capture-point offset, and lateral steps are shorter because the stance leg blocks the swing leg ([Yang et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2002.02991)). Taken together, **a 0.3 m/s push sits at or near Juan's physical limit**, especially sideways. The 52% sibling-run figure is therefore partly a property of the test, not only of the policy. With pushes every 2 to 4 s, a second push can also land before recovery steps finish.

Now add up a switch's latency budget. Debouncing takes 1 to 3 samples at 50 Hz, scaled from the 16 to 60 ms windows in Honda's fall-prediction patent ([US8554370B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8554370)). On top of that come one 20 ms policy period and a plausible 15 to 40 ms bus and servo delay. That consumes most of the 0.15 to 0.25 s window in which the recovery step must already be in flight. Detection lead times reported on full-size humanoids (0.2 to 1.6 s) should shrink roughly with √h on Juan, to tens or a few hundred milliseconds for push-induced falls ([Mungai et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.14546v2)). That is a scaling estimate, not a measurement. The conclusion holds either way: the step decision belongs inside the walker.

Juan's gait clock is a likely contributor to the falls. Van Marum et al. found that clock-based rewards make standing awkward and push the policy toward low foot velocities, "which hampers disturbance recovery." Double-support rewards penalize recovery steps. Their clock-free single-contact policy beat the clock-based one under pushes ([van Marum et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.19173v2)). With a rigid phase schedule, a push early in stance may wait up to half a gait cycle before the swing foot is "allowed" to move. On a 0.16 s time constant, a 0.1 to 0.2 s wait roughly doubles the capture-point error. You don't have to drop the clock, which would risk the gait you like. A middle path has precedent: let the policy output a phase-rate offset, as Duan et al. did on Cassie with gait periods of 0.65 to 1.2 s ([Duan et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.01807)). BITeno did the same with adaptive left and right clock frequencies, recovering within 1.5 to 2.0 s after a −1.2 m/s velocity change ([BITeno](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11201531/)). A softer variant is to down-weight the gait-contact reward for 0.5 to 1.0 s after a push. That idea is an inference by analogy; no paper reports numbers for it.

## Fine-tune juan_v3 with a push curriculum and a gait anchor

The training recipe that preserves the gait has three parts.

**Anchor to the original walker.** Fine-tune juan_v3 rather than training from scratch. Add a KL or behavior-cloning penalty toward a frozen copy of juan_v3, and make it adaptive the way OP3 did. OP3 regularized toward the teacher with weight 1 (pure imitation) while the predicted value was below a threshold, and dropped it to 0 (pure RL) above. The result was behaviors "more diverse, fluent, and robust than simply composing the skills" ([Haarnoja et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.13653)). In practice, apply the penalty heavily on nominal, undisturbed states and relax it in the 0.5 to 1 s after a push. The walker then keeps its gait where it already works and is free to improvise where it doesn't.

**Grow pushes adaptively.** Kumar's "region of attraction" method stores a maximum survivable push per direction (8 bins works well). Each episode samples a direction and a magnitude between 0.7–0.8× and 1.1× that bin's boundary. Every few iterations, a bin's boundary grows if success exceeds 90% and shrinks otherwise. Kumar reports 50% and 38% gains in backward and left push recovery over baselines ([Kumar thesis](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2201.01361)). This avoids two failure cases. Ramping force linearly over the episode, as the Atalante team did, "led to premature convergence to a suboptimal policy" ([Duburcq et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.01148)). Fixed large pushes early in training teach mostly falling. Mix instantaneous velocity kicks with 0.1 to 0.5 s force pulses, as Digit, Atalante and BDX use. Pulses are more physical and give the policy time to react mid-push ([van Marum et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.19173v2)). Scaled by mass from BDX's 90 to 150 N on 15.4 kg, a comparable pulse for Juan is roughly 10 to 17 N for 0.1 s; that figure is an estimate. Treat pushes beyond the computed 3-step limit as unrecoverable. Exclude them or terminate without a heavy penalty so they don't dominate learning. Track falls per direction, because lateral pushes are the likely binding case.

**Shape the reward around the capture point.** Give the critic the capture point, center-of-mass state and centroidal momentum as privileged inputs and shaping rewards, and give the actor only proprioception. On a Unitree H1-2, this let one policy discover ankle, hip and stepping strategies with 93.4% recovery over 10,000 simulated trials. Removing that structure made the stand-up skill fail to learn entirely ([Poddar et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.08619)). Juan already has an asymmetric actor-critic, so this is a small change. Concretely:

- an ICP term of the form exp(−‖ξ − p_support‖²/σ²), where ξ is the capture point and p_support is the center of the support foot or feet;
- torso roll and pitch terms and angular-rate terms;
- OP3's upright reward;
- a torque-peak penalty, which OP3 used so robots "rarely break knee gears."

Yang et al. showed stepping emerges unprogrammed from such rewards plus random pushes. Ankle, hip, foot-tilt and stepping strategies appeared in order of push size ([Yang et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2002.02991)).

Cassie adds two further levers. Task randomization (commands resampled every 1 to 15 s) is a separate source of robustness. A longer input/output history helps the policy adapt to contact events and dynamics shifts ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.16889)). Both matter for Juan's joystick goal. Resample velocity commands often, and include zero ("stand") commands about 10% of the time as Open Duck does ([Open Duck joystick.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/apirrone/Open_Duck_Playground/main/playground/open_duck_mini_v2/joystick.py)). Steering then arrives already robust. Lengthening the 3-frame history is worth an ablation if fine-tuning plateaus.

If fine-tuning cannot reach the robustness target without hurting the gait, the next step is distillation, not a runtime switch. Train a push-recovery expert under heavy pushes. Start its episodes from a reset buffer of states logged from juan_v3 in the 0.1 to 0.5 s after pushes, including ones that eventually fell, mixed about 70 to 80% with nominal states. Then distill juan_v3 and the expert into one student with DAgger and RL-fine-tune it. The reset-buffer method is a logical recommendation, not a published result. Your 10 to 15 degree tilt rule, ideally combined with tilt rate, belongs here: as the hand-coded label that picks which teacher to imitate during training, exactly as OP3 used a hand-coded upright check to choose between its get-up and soccer teachers. FIRM on the G1 shows the payoff of unifying. Its single distilled policy kept 93.2% success on uneven ground, where per-terrain specialists managed only 23.2% ([FIRM](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.07407v1)). Mixture-of-experts gating is not justified at this scale. The notes found no biped hardware example that uses it to combine a walker with a balance expert.

## Juan's real gap is likely latency, backlash and the IMU

The small-servo projects agree that actuator fidelity dominates sim-to-real. Open Duck's guide calls motor modeling the most important step and warns that if simulated motors differ from the real ones, "the policy won't work" ([Open Duck sim2real.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/apirrone/Open_Duck_Mini/v2/docs/sim2real.md)). Juan's actuator model (PD, torque-speed curve and latency) has the right structure. Three gaps remain.

**Parameters are not identified on the real servo.** No published model exists for the HTD-35H. Hiwonder's protocol shows no obvious way to read or set the servo's internal gains, so "kp" in simulation is an *effective* stiffness that must be fitted. The cheapest proven route is a pendulum bench: one clamped servo with a known arm and mass, driven by steps, 0.5 to 8 Hz chirps, and random 50 Hz target sequences, at both full and sagged battery voltage. Fit kp, damping, friction loss, armature and the torque-speed limit by replaying the bench in MuJoCo through your own actuator code. Rhoban's BAM tooling automates this and exports to MuJoCo ([BAM](https://github.com/rimim/bam)). ToddlerBot's version adds back-driving and spin-down tests and reached **1.3 degrees mean tracking error**, a good target ([ToddlerBot](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00893v4)). Also send `SERVO_MOVE_TIME_WRITE` with a time of 0 or exactly 20 ms. A longer time makes the servo interpolate internally, which simulation does not model ([Hiwonder protocol](https://engineering.purdue.edu/477grp4/Files/refs/motors_manual.pdf)).

**Backlash is not modeled.** Disney models backlash in the measured joint angle and randomizes it per episode ([Grandia et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204)). Pollen's microduck places a ±1 degree passive hinge in series with every servo ([microduck_rl](https://gitee.com/ncnynl/microduck_rl)). Open Duck's headline training task is literally `flat_terrain_backlash` ([Open Duck Playground](https://github.com/apirrone/Open_Duck_Playground)). Backlash plus delay erodes the phase margin of exactly the fast ankle and hip corrections that push recovery relies on. A policy trained without them learns high-gain reactions that wobble or overshoot on hardware.

**Latency is probably underestimated.** Wire time alone at 115200 baud is about 6.9 ms to write 8 positions and about 9.7 ms to read them, one request and reply each. USB-serial polling can add up to 16 ms per round trip on an FTDI adapter at its default latency timer. The notes estimate end-to-end observation-to-motion delay at **15 to 40 ms**, above Juan's 5 to 19 ms range. Measure it by commanding steps and timestamping the first read-back change. Then randomize per episode over about 0.5 to 1.5× the median, extended to the 95th percentile, and model the roughly 0.87 ms stagger between successive servo writes. Open Duck randomizes action delay and IMU delay independently by 0 to 2 control steps, and sets the USB latency timer to 1 ms with a udev rule ([Open Duck Runtime](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/apirrone/Open_Duck_Mini_Runtime/v2/README.md)). If reads still eat the 20 ms budget, pipeline them (write, then read for the next tick) or move bus I/O to a microcontroller.

The BNO055 needs care. Its fusion output runs at a fixed 100 Hz, and magnetometer calibration events cause documented heading jumps ([Bosch datasheet](https://www.bosch-sensortec.com/media/boschsensortec/downloads/datasheets/bst-bno055-ds000.pdf); [Bosch community](https://community.bosch-sensortec.com/t5/MEMS-sensors-forum/BNO-055-calibrate-using-horizontal-plane-rotations-only-And/td-p/20760)). Run it in IMU mode (accelerometer and gyro fusion, no magnetometer). Feed the policy the fused gravity vector plus raw gyro, and never magnetometer yaw. In simulation, randomize:

- IMU delay of 0 to 2 steps;
- gyro noise around 0.05 to 0.1 rad/s, plus a small per-episode bias;
- a per-episode mounting tilt bias of 2 to 3 degrees, matching OP3's ±2 degree IMU-orientation randomization ([Haarnoja et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.13653)).

Copy the remaining cheap randomizations too:

- joint-calibration offsets of ±0.02 to 0.03 rad (BDX, Open Duck);
- kp scaled by 0.9 to 1.1×;
- battery-voltage sag;
- a per-step target rate limit at the servo's real speed (about 5.8 rad/s × 0.02 s ≈ 0.12 rad per step), as Open Duck does at 5.24 rad/s ([Open Duck joystick.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/apirrone/Open_Duck_Playground/main/playground/open_duck_mini_v2/joystick.py)).

The EMA action filter must be identical in training and deployment, as in OP3 and BDX. Watch servo temperature. ToddlerBot's longest no-fall run was 19 minutes, after which motor heating pushed it out of distribution and falls increased ([ToddlerBot](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00893v4)).

Here is the runtime supervisor that *is* worth building. It is a conservative fall-mitigation and safety layer, not a recoverer. When tilt and tilt rate show the fall is unrecoverable, it switches to a crouch-and-freeze mode like BDX's "motion stop," which forces standing and then locks joints with high gains after 0.5 s ([Grandia et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204)). Past roughly 30 to 40 degrees, or on loop overruns or bus failures, it unloads torque to protect the gears. Give it separate enter and exit thresholds, an 80%-of-window debounce, and a high-confidence trigger. Raising a learned predictor's threshold from 0.5 to 0.9 eliminated false alarms on Digit at little cost in lead time ([Mungai et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.14546v2)). The PPO critic's value is a free fall-likelihood signal to log alongside tilt, but nothing has validated it as a trigger on bipeds. On a legs-only, armless robot, a learned get-up like OP3's may not be physically possible. Durable bumpers and fast part replacement matter more: ToddlerBot broke after up to 7 falls.

## The ordered plan for Juan

| Step | Action | Why it comes here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Evaluate juan_v3 under the same randomization + 0.3 m/s push test as the sibling run. Log falls per push direction and gait phase, with a 1.5 to 2 s settle gap between pushes. | Juan_v3 has never been measured under this test. Per-direction data shows whether lateral pushes or clock timing drive the falls. |
| 2 | Bench-identify one HTD-35H (BAM or ToddlerBot style). Measure end-to-end latency and backlash. Update the actuator model and randomization ranges. | Highest-leverage sim-to-real fix. Robustness trained against the wrong servo model will not transfer. |
| 3 | Fine-tune juan_v3 with an adaptive per-direction push curriculum, the corrected randomization, ICP and upright terms in the critic and reward, frequent command resampling, and an adaptive KL/BC anchor to frozen juan_v3. | One deployed network that keeps the gait. Direct precedent from OP3, BDX and Cassie. |
| 4 | If stepping is still too slow, let the policy modulate the clock's phase rate, or relax the contact reward after pushes. | Clocks suppress recovery steps. Phase-rate control keeps your gait structure. |
| 5 | Only if 3 and 4 plateau: train a push expert from walker reset states and distill with DAgger, using tilt and tilt rate as the teacher label. | Modular training with no runtime handoff. |
| 6 | Deploy with a conservative safe-fall/freeze supervisor, a torque-off watchdog, temperature and voltage monitoring, and a harness-first test ladder. | Damage control for falls the policy cannot prevent. |

## Conclusion

Your 10 to 15 degree tilt threshold is better used as a label than as a switch. On a robot with a 0.16 s balance time constant, a tilt threshold arrives after the step decision was already due. Its value is in telling the trainer which teacher to imitate, and in telling the runtime when to stop trying and protect the hardware. That turns the architecture question into a training-and-fidelity question. Expect the biggest gains to come from teaching juan_v3 to step out of its clock when pushed, and from making the simulated servo, bus and IMU as slow and sloppy as the real ones.

Two uncertainties should shape expectations. First, no source quantifies push recovery on a hobby biped this small, so Juan's true ceiling is unknown until juan_v3 is measured and the servo is identified. The capture-point table implies that 0.3 m/s pushes, especially sideways, may never fall to near-zero failures, whatever the architecture. Second, no study directly compares switching against a unified policy on biped hardware. The recommendation rests on the consistent direction of the indirect evidence, and that evidence lines up with what the hobby projects closest to Juan actually ship.
