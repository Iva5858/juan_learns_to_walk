# Measure Juan's servo bus before trusting the sim

Juan's electronics are adequate for a 2 kg, 50 Hz biped, but three of them behave quite differently from the current simulation, and the power wiring needs four changes before assembly. The servos matter most. Hiwonder publishes one operating point for the HTD-35H (3.43 Nm stall and 5.8 rad/s no-load at 11.1 V) and calls the torque "a reference value, not a recommended continuous-load rating." The protocol exposes no gains, velocity, current or sync-read, and runs at a fixed 115200 baud. Reading all eight positions through the USB BusLinker likely takes 20 to 30 ms, which is more than the whole control period. The simulation's latency range (5 to 19 ms, silently capped below one 20 ms step) therefore probably covers only the optimistic end of reality. The BNO055's white noise is about fifty times smaller than the noise Juan already injects. Its per-episode bias and its fusion behaviour are not modelled at all, and they matter more. On the power side, the LM2596 is marginal for a Pi 4 with a camera. The 100 µF capacitor is too small. The servo current should not run through the BusLinker. And a factory voltage limit inherited from 7.4 V servos could cut torque on a freshly charged 3S pack. The electronics weigh roughly 0.85 to 0.95 kg, about half the robot. That leaves no slack for heavier parts, so weigh every one and put the real masses in the MJCF. The rest of this report gives each part's specs and real-world behaviour, then three decision tables: hardware changes, bench measurements and simulation settings.

## Electronics make up about half of Juan's mass

The eight servos alone weigh 512 g, more than a quarter of a 1.75 to 2 kg robot. With both batteries, the Pi and the wiring, the electronics come to roughly 0.85 to 0.95 kg. That leaves about 0.8 to 1.1 kg for brackets, feet and torso structure. The two batteries (about 230 to 250 g together) are the largest single masses you can choose where to put. Their placement sets the torso centre of mass more than anything else, which is why the simulation should randomize torso CoM, as Open Duck does (±0.05 m per axis, torso mass ±0.1 kg) ([Open Duck randomize.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/apirrone/Open_Duck_Playground/main/playground/common/randomize.py)). A static torque check shows why the servo mass is justified, and also where it runs short. My arithmetic: in single support the full ~19.6 N body weight acts on one leg. The 3.43 Nm stall torque can therefore hold the body's line of action up to about 17 cm from a joint axis. The roughly 1.5 Nm the servos can sustain without overheating (see the next section) holds it only within about 7.5 cm. Deep crouches are where the servos will overheat.

## The HTD-35H publishes one operating point and hides the rest

Everything Hiwonder states about the HTD-35H fits on one line: 35 kg·cm and 0.18 s/60° at 11.1 V, 9 to 12.6 V, 100 mA no-load, 3 A stall, 64 g, 0.24° per count, and "accuracy" of 0.2° (another listing says 0.3°) ([openelab](https://openelab.io/products/htd-35h-bus-servo)). No torque or speed curve versus voltage is published, and neither are deadband, backlash, temperature range or position-sensor type. The listing itself warns that **torque is "a reference value, not a recommended continuous-load rating"** and tells you to size the supply around the 3 A stall current. Do not confuse the part with the HTS-35H, a different 35 kg·cm servo with brass gears and a single output shaft ([openelab HTS-35H](https://openelab.io/a/s/products/hts-35h-bus-servo)).

A simple DC-motor model fills the gaps; this is my inference, not Hiwonder data. Stall torque and no-load speed both scale roughly with supply voltage:

| Pack state | Bus voltage | Stall torque | No-load speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full | 12.6 V | ~3.9 Nm | ~6.6 rad/s |
| Under load near cutoff | 10.2 V | ~3.15 Nm | ~5.3 rad/s |
| Spec floor | 9.0 V | ~2.8 Nm | ~4.7 rad/s |

From the 3 A stall figure, the model implies about 3.7 Ω effective resistance, a torque constant of about 1.14 Nm/A and a back-EMF constant of about 1.9 V·s/rad at the output. The mismatch between those two constants points to roughly 60% gearbox efficiency. That is close to the ~58% ToddlerBot inferred for its Dynamixels ([ToddlerBot](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00893v4)). With stall protection and thermal cut-off, realistic *continuous* torque is perhaps 30 to 50% of stall, about 1 to 1.7 Nm.

The protocol is the shared LewanSoul/Hiwonder bus protocol: 115200 8N1, half-duplex on a single wire. Frames are `0x55 0x55 ID Len Cmd Params Checksum`, and commands 1 to 36 cover move, offset, angle limits, voltage and temperature limits, temperature, voltage and position reads, motor mode, load/unload and LEDs ([austin-bowen/lewansoul-servo-bus](https://github.com/austin-bowen/lewansoul-servo-bus)). What the command list leaves out matters just as much. **There are no P/D gains, no torque or current limit, no velocity register, no load reading, no live fault status, no sync-read and no baud-rate command.** Sim kp and kd therefore have to be *fitted*, not read. LOAD_OR_UNLOAD is the only torque knob. The time argument of MOVE_TIME_WRITE is the only speed knob. And the bus stays at 115200 for good. The library's "velocity" is just two position reads with a sleep between them ([austin-bowen](https://github.com/austin-bowen/lewansoul-servo-bus)). At 0.24° per count, a finite-difference velocity at 50 Hz moves in steps of about 0.21 rad/s per count. That is too coarse to be a useful policy input, so prefer action history.

Two protection settings can cause falls. In the same library, VIN_LIMIT is clipped to **4500–12000 mV**, a range inherited from the 7.4 V LX-16A. Its docstring says the servos are "powered off" outside the limit and that the setting persists ([austin-bowen](https://github.com/austin-bowen/lewansoul-servo-bus)). If your HTD-35Hs ship with a 12.0 V ceiling, a freshly charged 12.6 V pack, or a back-driving voltage spike during a fall, will make them go limp. Read the limit (command 23) before anything else. TEMP_MAX_LIMIT defaults to 85 °C and also unloads the motor. Because the servo reports no live fault status, the Pi cannot ask why a servo went limp ([austin-bowen](https://github.com/austin-bowen/lewansoul-servo-bus)). For a biped, every protection trip means a fall. ToddlerBot found that rising motor temperature alone pushed its robot out of distribution and increased falls ([ToddlerBot](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00893v4)).

The library also has two traps. It reports temperature in **Fahrenheit by default**. And leaving its context manager **powers all servos off by default**, which drops a standing robot ([austin-bowen](https://github.com/austin-bowen/lewansoul-servo-bus)). Several protocol details come from memory of the official PDF and could not be re-verified, so check each one against a copy you open yourself: the servo replies only to reads; broadcast ID 254 works for writes; and MOVE_TIME_WAIT_WRITE to each servo followed by one broadcast MOVE_START would start all eight servos on the same byte.

## Reading eight servos through USB likely overruns 20 ms

The "BusLinker" name covers two boards. The small 25 × 40 mm V2.x TTL/USB debug board is the one sold with LX and HTD servos. One retailer lists V2.4 as **CH340C-based with a 6–8.4 V input** ([sigmanortec](https://sigmanortec.ro/en/ttl-usb-module-buslinker-v24-for-serial-servomotors)). The newer V3.0 takes 5 to 14 V and runs up to 1 Mbps, but it targets Hiwonder's magnetic-encoder servos and still tells you not to treat the USB cable as the power source for a high-load system ([openelab BusLinker V3](https://openelab.io/products/buslinker-v3-servo-board)). Neither board has a published current rating. Run `lsusb`: `1a86:7523` means a CH340. The CH340 has no FTDI-style `latency_timer` to tune. The half-duplex circuit echoes every transmitted byte back onto RX, so the driver must discard the echo or its frame parser will drift out of alignment ([EtcetFelix](https://github.com/EtcetFelix/HiWonder-BusLinker-servo-bus)). The same fork reports that replies through BusLinker V2.5 "are different than HiWonder's documented protocol," without saying how.

The timing arithmetic is the core problem. At 115200 baud each byte takes 86.8 µs. Writing eight 10-byte move frames back to back takes **about 6.9 ms**. Each position read is a 6-byte request plus an 8-byte reply, so eight reads take **about 9.7 ms of wire time alone**. Each read is also a blocking USB round trip of roughly 1 to 3 ms on a CH340, plus Python overhead. That puts eight sequential reads at **about 20 to 30 ms**, longer than the 20 ms control period. This is an estimate; no published benchmark exists for this servo family, so it is the first thing to measure. The only empirical hint is example code that waits 10 ms between a move and a read ([tve/lx16a-servo](https://github.com/tve/lx16a-servo)).

Three remedies work, in increasing order of effort. First, send all eight writes in one `write()` call and read only four positions per tick, alternating halves. Each joint reading is then 20 to 40 ms stale, which the simulation must model. Second, replace the BusLinker with the Pi's PL011 UART plus a 74HC126-type tri-state buffer. That removes USB polling and makes eight reads plausible in about 10 to 12 ms. Third, put an RP2040 or ESP32 on the bus to poll continuously and stream packed state to the Pi, which pays the USB latency once per tick instead of eight times. Whichever you choose, run the bus in its own thread so the latest state is always ready. Use 3 to 5 ms read timeouts rather than the library's 1 s, resync on `0x55 0x55`, reuse the last good value when a read fails, and trip a watchdog after N consecutive failures.

## The BNO055's bias and fusion behaviour matter more than its noise

The Bosch datasheet (Rev 1.8) settles the IMU numbers. In **IMU mode (OPR_MODE 0x08, accelerometer plus gyro)** the fused outputs run at 100 Hz with no magnetometer, so heading cannot jump. In fusion modes the sensor configuration is fixed: accelerometer ±4 g at 62.5 Hz bandwidth, gyro ±2000 °/s at 32 Hz ([Bosch datasheet](https://www.bosch-sensortec.com/media/boschsensortec/downloads/datasheets/bst-bno055-ds000.pdf)). The raw gyro's output noise is **0.1 °/s rms (about 0.002 rad/s)**, and accelerometer noise density is 150 µg/√Hz. Juan's current gyro noise of 0.1 rad/s is therefore about fifty times the sensor's noise, and gravity noise of 0.03 is about twenty times it. Those values are really modelling vibration, impacts and unmodelled dynamics. That is defensible, but it is not sensor noise. The datasheet terms that matter are the ones the simulation omits:

| Error term | Datasheet value | Effect on gravity or gyro |
|---|---|---|
| Accelerometer zero-g offset | **±80 mg typical, ±150 mg max** | 4.6–8.5° of tilt error if uncalibrated |
| Package alignment error | 0.5° typical, 2° max | Constant tilt offset |
| Gyro zero-rate offset | **±1 °/s typical, ±3 °/s max** | Per-episode gyro bias |

The datasheet's own notes describe fusion behaviour that matters to a walker. Background auto-calibration **"cannot be disabled"** and overwrites restored offsets. During motion the filter ignores the accelerometer and integrates the gyro for pitch and roll. Under sustained acceleration it can mistake that acceleration for gravity ([Bosch datasheet](https://www.bosch-sensortec.com/media/boschsensortec/downloads/datasheets/bst-bno055-ds000.pdf)). One car-mounted user saw pitch and roll swing up to ±20° under sustained cornering ([Bosch community](https://community.bosch-sensortec.com/t5/MEMS-sensors-forum/BNO055-Pitch-Roll-Readings-Under-High-Centripetal-Acceleration/m-p/7580)). That is a single anecdote at about 0.3 to 0.6 g, not a biped measurement, and the datasheet gives no fused-attitude accuracy and no latency. The output is 0 to 10 ms old at 100 Hz, plus unknown filter lag, plus the I2C read time.

Plugging the BNO055 into the Pi's I2C header runs into a well-documented bug. **The BNO055 clock-stretches, and the Pi's hardware I2C mishandles stretching right after an ACK.** A scope study found a nominal 100 kHz bus actually running at 62.5 kHz, with **about 6% of reads corrupted** ([softsolder](https://softsolder.com/2018/09/04/raspberry-pi-i2c-bus-timing-vs-bno055-clock-stretching/)). Pi 4 users report `None` values and −92 °C temperatures ([issue #58](https://github.com/adafruit/Adafruit_CircuitPython_BNO055/issues/58), [issue #67](https://github.com/adafruit/Adafruit_CircuitPython_BNO055/issues/67)). Adafruit's fix, slowing the bus to 10 kHz, is unusable at 50 Hz. A 32-byte burst then takes about 32 ms, and the Adafruit property API doubles the transaction count, because every property first re-reads the mode register ([adafruit_bno055.py](https://github.com/adafruit/Adafruit_CircuitPython_BNO055/blob/main/adafruit_bno055.py)). Better routes are the kernel's bit-banged `dtoverlay=i2c-gpio`, which handles clock stretching ([Adafruit example](https://github.com/adafruit/Adafruit_CircuitPython_BNO055/blob/main/examples/bno055_i2c-gpio_simpletest.py)), or the BNO055's UART mode on a PL011 (about 3.3 ms for a 32-byte read). Use the library only for setup. In the loop, do one `smbus2` burst read of registers 0x14 to 0x33, run plausibility gates (|g| between 9.0 and 10.6 m/s²), and hold the last good sample when a read fails.

## The Pi has compute to spare but is short on power and cooling

The Pi 4 needs **5.1 V at 3 A**. It draws about 0.6 A at idle and 1.25 A under full stress, the camera adds 250 mA, and the board flags undervoltage below **4.63 V** ([RPi docs](https://www.raspberrypi.com/documentation/computers/raspberry-pi.html)). Thermal throttling begins at 80 °C. A bare board under stress first throttled after 65 to 177 s depending on firmware ([RPi thermal testing](https://www.raspberrypi.com/news/thermal-testing-raspberry-pi-4/)), and a closed case idles around 65 °C. A sealed torso will throttle without a heatsink and fan, and throttling silently changes loop timing. The GPIO 14/15 UART defaults to the **mini UART**, whose baud rate follows the core clock. Any device UART therefore belongs on a PL011 (`dtoverlay=uart3` and similar; UART2–5 are PL011s) ([RPi UART docs](https://www.raspberrypi.com/documentation/computers/configuration.html#configure-uarts)). Using `disable-bt` instead would cost you the Bluetooth gamepad.

Compute is not the constraint. Open Duck runs its ONNX walker on a Pi Zero 2 W ([Open Duck](https://github.com/apirrone/Open_Duck_Mini)), and a 3 × 256 MLP should take about 50 to 300 µs on a Pi 4 (an estimate to benchmark). Stock-kernel scheduling jitter is about 300 µs worst case versus 93 µs with PREEMPT_RT ([LeMaRiva](https://lemariva.com/blog/2019/09/raspberry-pi-4b-preempt-rt-kernel-419y-performance-test)), small next to 20 ms. The real jitter sources are blocking serial and I2C I/O, Python garbage collection, throttling and Wi-Fi interrupt load. The camera is cheap if it uses the Pi 4's hardware encoders, Picamera2's `H264Encoder` or `MJPEGEncoder`. The software `JpegEncoder` defaults to four threads and would compete with the control loop ([picamera2 encoders](https://github.com/raspberrypi/picamera2/tree/main/picamera2/encoders)). Keep the CSI ribbon under about 25 cm ([Adafruit via buyzero](https://buyzero.de/products/adafruit-csi-or-dsi-cable-extender-thingy-for-raspberry-pi)) and route it away from moving joints.

## The power design needs four fixes before assembly

The servo pack is fine. The simulation's 2.7 to 3.7 A mean and 9 to 10 A peaks are only about 4.5C on a 2200 mAh pack. Any reputable ≥25C pack covers even a 24 A all-stall fault, and an expensive 75C pack only adds weight. Assuming about 80% usable capacity, expect **roughly 25 to 35 minutes of walking** per charge (my arithmetic). The risk is voltage, not current. Near empty, a resting 10.8 V pack sags to about 10.3 V at 10 A, leaving only about 1 V of margin above the servos' 9 V floor. Warn at about 10.5 V under load and sit down at about 10.2 V (3.4 V/cell). The 650 mAh logic pack runs a Pi drawing about 5 W for **55 to 60 minutes**, so it outlasts the servo pack ([PiDramble](https://pidramble.com/node/27)). But the regulator keeps drawing current as the pack drains, so an unattended Pi will over-discharge it below 3.0 V/cell, the point at which a pack should be retired ([Rutgers](https://construction.rutgers.edu/rehs/lithium-battery-guidance)).

The four changes are as follows. **First, keep servo current out of the BusLinker.** The reported 6 to 8.4 V input rating of V2.4 contradicts a 12.6 V pack, and no current rating exists for the terminals or traces. **Second, replace the LM2596.** The IC is rated at 3 A ([TI](https://www.ti.com/product/LM2596)), but cheap modules are comfortable at only about 1.5 to 2 A continuous ([Microcontrollerslab](https://microcontrollerslab.com/?p=44740)). One "LM2596" module turned out to be a clone switching at 55 kHz instead of 150 kHz ([Hackaday.io](https://hackaday.io/project/168286/log/173866)). A Pololu D36V50F5 (5 V, 3.5 to 8 A, soft-start and protection) gives more than twice the headroom ([Pololu](https://www.pololu.com/product/4091)). **Third, upsize the capacitor.** Pololu measured plug-in LC ringing reaching about 25 V on a 13 V battery with short leads ([Pololu LC spikes](https://www.pololu.com/docs/0J16/all)). On a 12.6 V rail feeding 12.6 V-rated servos, a 100 µF part damps too little. A 16 V capacitor is under-rated as well. **Fourth, add a servo-power switch independent of the Pi.** A MOSFET switch cut Pololu's 40 V spike to under 20 V, and mechanical contacts bounce and spike repeatedly ([Pololu](https://www.pololu.com/docs/0J16/all)). Keep the two-battery design: it isolates the Pi from 10 to 24 A transients. Tie the grounds at one point. The BusLinker's USB GND already does this, so a direct-UART design needs exactly one added ground wire.

## Recommendations

### Hardware changes before assembly

| Change | Detail | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Servo power distribution | XT60 → 15–20 A inline fuse → MOSFET or anti-spark switch → small distribution bus, then one 18–20 AWG feed per leg (4 servos per chain). The BusLinker carries signal + GND only. | BusLinker V2.4 is reported at 6–8.4 V input with unrated traces; the first link of a chain carries every downstream servo's current |
| Bulk capacitance | 470–1000 µF, ≥25 V, low-ESR at the bus entry; optional 100–220 µF at the far end of each leg chain; optional 15 V TVS (SMBJ13A/15A) | Plug-in ringing and regenerated energy during back-driven falls; 12.6 V already equals the servo maximum |
| 5 V regulator | Replace the LM2596 with a Pololu D36V50F5 or D24V50F5 (or a 5 V ≥5 A UBEC); short ≤20 cm USB-C pigtail. If you keep the LM2596: heatsink it, set 5.15–5.25 V measured at the Pi under load, lock the trimpot with glue | Pi + camera bursts above 2 A; undervoltage flag at 4.63 V |
| Battery monitoring | Balance-lead buzzer (3.5 V/cell) on **both** packs; optionally an ADS1115 divider for the logic pack so the Pi can shut down cleanly | The logic pack is easy to over-discharge; servo VIN_READ covers only the servo pack |
| Pi cooling and venting | Heatsink + small fan or fan shim; vent the torso | A closed case idles ~65 °C; throttling at 80 °C changes timing |
| IMU wiring | Plan for `i2c-gpio` on spare GPIOs, or BNO055 UART mode on `uart3`. Mount the board rigidly near the torso CoM, aligned to torso axes, with vibration-isolating tape | Pi hardware I2C corrupts BNO055 reads; mounting tilt becomes a bias |
| Servo bus path | Keep the BusLinker for bring-up, but leave room and a PL011 UART (or an RP2040) for a direct bus if timing measurements fail | 8 USB round trips likely exceed 20 ms |
| Mechanics | Use the servo's dual output shaft with U-brackets; route the camera ribbon away from moving joints; padded, strapped battery mounts | Hiwonder warns about side load; ribbon and pack damage in falls |

### Bench measurements

| Measure | How | Feeds into |
|---|---|---|
| Board ID and servo limits | `lsusb`; read VIN_LIMIT (cmd 23), TEMP_MAX_LIMIT (25) and angle limits on every servo. If VIN max ≈ 12.0 V, try raising it to 12.6–13 V and set min ≈ 9.5 V; set per-joint ANGLE_LIMIT | Avoids limp-on-full-pack falls |
| Bus timing | Log `time.perf_counter_ns` around a 1-write + 8-read cycle over 10,000 ticks (p50/p99/max); a logic analyzer on the bus gives servo turnaround and inter-frame gaps. Repeat with 4 reads per tick | Decides BusLinker vs direct UART vs MCU, and the obs-staleness model |
| End-to-end latency | Command small steps at 50 Hz on a lightly loaded servo; timestamp from command to the first read-back change ≥2 counts, over hundreds of trials | Sim action-delay distribution |
| Servo model ID | Pendulum bench (0.2–0.5 kg at 10–15 cm). Steps of 5/15/30°, 0.5–8 Hz chirps, random 50 Hz targets, at ~12.4 V and ~10.8 V, with time = 0 **and** time = 20 ms. Fit kp, damping, frictionloss, armature, τ_max and q̇_max by replaying in MuJoCo through Juan's actuator code (BAM or ToddlerBot style; target ~1.3° mean error) | Replaces datasheet-derived servo parameters |
| Backlash and deadband | Hold position, wiggle the horn against a long pointer or dial gauge while reading POS_READ; command 1-count increments to find the smallest one that moves | Backlash randomization range |
| Current and voltage sag | Clamp meter or inline shunt: one servo at stall (verify 3 A); 8 servos standing in the nominal crouch; bus voltage at the servo end during 10 A peaks on a near-empty pack | Brownout margin, cutoff threshold, wire gauge |
| Thermal and stall protection | Hold a servo against a stop at 11.1 V while logging TEMP_READ, current and time until unload; hold the standing crouch for 10 min and log temperatures; re-run steps on a hot servo | Continuous-torque limit, run-length rules, low-strength sim tail |
| IMU bias and noise | Robot static on a level surface for 5 min: log gravity, gyro, CALIB_STAT. Measure mean tilt vs a level reference (mounting bias) and gyro mean (bias), then repeat after power cycles | Per-episode tilt and gyro bias ranges |
| IMU bus reliability | Count implausible or failed reads per 10,000 on each bus option (hw I2C 100/400 kHz, i2c-gpio, UART) | Bus choice; dropped-sample rate in sim |
| IMU dynamic error | Shake or tilt-step the robot (or a hand-held torso) while logging gravity; compare to the static gravity afterwards and to a stepped servo angle on a jig | Whether to use a filtered-gravity model in sim |
| Pi power and thermals | 5 V at the Pi header under full load + camera; `vcgencmd get_throttled` and SoC temperature for 20 min in the closed torso | Regulator choice, cooling |
| Control-loop jitter | Full stack running (bus, IMU, policy, camera, Wi-Fi), per-tick timestamps for I/O, compute and total | Latency tails; whether PREEMPT_RT is worth building |

### Simulation settings

| Setting | Current | Change to | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Action latency | 5–19 ms, silently capped below 20 ms | A delay buffer at physics-substep resolution that can exceed one step. Until measured, sample 10–60 ms per episode (Open Duck uses 0–3 steps); after measuring, 0.5–1.5× the median, extended to p95, with small per-step jitter. | Setpoint profile | Implicit step | Match the deployed MOVE_TIME_WRITE time: a step for time = 0, or a linear ramp from previous to new target over 20 ms (update ctrl each substep) for time = 20 ms | Firmware interpolates for nonzero time |
| Torque-speed curve | 3.43 Nm / 5.8 rad/s linear, strength and speed ×0.85–1.13 | Keep the line, but draw one voltage factor per episode and scale stall torque **and** no-load speed together (≈0.92–1.14 for 10.2–12.6 V). Keep a low tail to 0.85 for heat and weak units, plus ±5% per-joint spread | DC-motor scaling ∝ V; the current independent draws create impossible combinations |

### Onboard software plan

The runtime should be four processes or threads with clear ownership. A **servo-bus thread** owns the serial port. It sends all eight move frames in one `write()` at the start of each tick, reads positions on the measured schedule, swaps in one VIN_READ or TEMP_READ every 25th tick for health monitoring, discards echo, and resyncs on errors. An **IMU thread** owns the I2C (or UART) bus. It does one burst read every 10 ms and publishes a timestamped latest sample. A **control process** is pinned to an isolated core (`isolcpus=3`, `chrt -f 80`, performance governor, `gc` disabled in the loop, absolute-deadline sleeps). It assembles the observation, runs the MLP single-threaded in NumPy or ONNX Runtime, applies the same action filter and rate limit as training, and logs per-tick timings. The **camera and teleop process** runs on the remaining cores: hardware MJPEG or H.264, and a Bluetooth gamepad through `evdev` with a dead-man button. A **supervisor** layer broadcasts LOAD_OR_UNLOAD = 0 to ID 254 when it sees tilt beyond about 30 to 40°, a loop overrun over 100 ms, repeated bus failures, servo temperature over about 65 °C or bus voltage under 10.2 V. It also detects silent servo protection trips by noticing position diverging from target. At power-up, read the positions first, then ramp to the stand pose over 1 to 2 s, so eight servos do not lunge at near-stall current together. Log `vcgencmd get_throttled` and flag any run with bits 0, 2, 16 or 18 set ([RPi vcgencmd](https://www.raspberrypi.com/documentation/computers/os.html#vcgencmd)).

## Conclusion

On Juan the information path is a bigger constraint than the actuators' strength. The HTD-35H is strong enough for a 2 kg biped, but its 115200-baud, read-one-at-a-time protocol makes observation freshness a design choice rather than a given. The simulation's sub-step latency cap encodes an assumption the hardware probably cannot meet. That reframes the sim-to-real work. The decisive numbers are not torque and speed, which already sit inside a reasonable randomization band. They are the measured bus round trip, backlash and IMU bias, none of which the current model represents. A policy trained to tolerate 20 to 40 ms of staleness, a degree of play and a few degrees of tilt bias will carry over to hardware better than one trained more precisely against an idealized servo.

The other lesson is that every built-in protection on these parts fails toward going limp: the servo unloads on voltage, temperature or stall, and the Pi throttles on heat or undervoltage. On a biped, each of those protections causes a fall, and the defaults were not chosen for a 3S walking robot. Some uncertainty remains. No published bench data exists for the HTD-35H's timing, backlash or thermal behaviour. Several protocol details rest on an unverified reading of the official PDF. The BNO055's dynamic error during foot impacts has never been measured on a biped. A day of bench work turns each of these from a guess into a parameter, and it is worth doing before more PPO training.
